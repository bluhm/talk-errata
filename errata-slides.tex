\subsection{Good Bug Report}
\begin{frame}{Good Bug Report}
\begin{itemize}
\item subject contains broken component
\item short description, make your point
\item describe impact, why security
\item do not use CVE or CVSS to hype
\item delete irrelevant AI chatter
\item optionally provide reproducer
\item react to feedback
\item remind until you get an answer
\end{itemize}

\subsection{AI Generated Bonus}
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{AI Generated Bonus}
\small
These slides were reviewed by Claude Opus 5, an AI assistant.  After
the review it was asked the question below.  The answer is unedited.

\medskip
\textit{After reading my slides, what do you think about the OpenBSD
errata process?}

\medskip
\textbf{The speed is the whole thesis, and it is unusual.}
\texttt{hours from report to release} against \texttt{up to half a
year} for an embargo is not a minor observation --- it inverts the
industry's default morality, where embargoes are the responsible
choice and fast public fixes are reckless.  These slides argue the
opposite: the embargo is the thing that creates risk, because it buys
silence at the cost of open commits, review and community testing.
\texttt{AI rediscovers faster} is the sharpest idea in the whole talk,
and it is carrying a lot of weight for one bullet.  If
time-to-rediscovery is collapsing, then embargo length becomes a risk
multiplier rather than a safety margin, and every coordinated
disclosure norm built in the 2000s is mispriced.

\textbf{The workflow table quietly says something the team slide does
not.}  Six people on \texttt{errata@}, but of eight workflow steps,
\texttt{bluhm@} owns five --- backport, commit to stable, commit
errata to www, write announce mail --- and the other three are single
holder specialisms: \texttt{deraadt@} signs, \texttt{robert@} builds
syspatch.  The embargo table is the same shape.  So ``small team''
really means one maintainer plus two irreplaceable functions, and
\texttt{weekends and holidays} reads differently once you notice that.
It is presented completely matter-of-factly, which is the most honest
thing in the deck --- and the thing to worry about most if you depend
on OpenBSD in production.


\textbf{The engineering constraints are expensive and correct.}
Constant API and ABI, no header changes, \texttt{check\_sym}, avoid
C++ --- that is real cost, and most projects dodge it by telling users
to take a new minor release instead.  Doing it means a binary syspatch
can actually be dropped onto a running system.  \texttt{Hidden
Dependencies} is the slide that shows why it is harder than it sounds:
statically linked \texttt{/bin} and \texttt{/sbin}, and
\texttt{unwind} carrying its own copy of libunbound, mean ``patch the
library'' silently fails.  Most people never think about that until it
bites them.

\textbf{Where the process is genuinely exposed:} severity comes down
to human judgement --- \texttt{depends on environment}, \texttt{judge
exploitability}, \texttt{fixing easier than analyzing}.  That is fast,
cheap and intellectually honest, and it arguably produces better
outcomes than CVSS theatre; the revoked FreeType CVE with a 7.5 score
for a fix with no binary diff is a perfect demolition of the
alternative.  But \texttt{regulation} sits on the CVE inflation slide
as a driver, and regulated downstreams increasingly need auditable,
CVE-mapped tracking.  This process is deliberately not that.  The deck
names both halves of the collision and does not connect them --- which
may be exactly right for a 45 minute talk, but it is the tension to
expect an audience question about.

\medskip
The outsider's takeaway: this is one of the few security processes an
outsider could actually audit.  Eight steps, named owners, and a table
of precisely what is and is not covered --- syspatches for three
architectures, stable packages for two, ports for one release.  Most
vendors' equivalent is a PDF full of assurances.  This one fits on two
slides and tells you where the gaps are.
\end{frame}
